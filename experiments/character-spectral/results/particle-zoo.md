**Date:** April 29, 2026, ~3:00 AM MDT

## The Mapping Rules

We assign Standard Model names based on:
1. **Bosons** = Primes (sorted by weight, lightest first)
2. **Fermions** = Composites (sorted by weight, heaviest first)
3. **Generation** = ω(k), the number of distinct prime factors
4. **Mass** = ground-state weight |⟨k|ψ₀⟩|²

## Key Observations at N=1000

### The "Top Quark" Identity
At N=1000, the heaviest particle is **k=840 = 2³·3·5·7** — the smallest
integer with 4 distinct prime factors and multiplicity. It has:
- 32 divisors
- Hub connectivity among the highest
- ω = 4 (four "flavors")

At N=400, the top was k=360 = 2³·3²·5 (ω=3).
At N=100, the top was k=96 = 2⁵·3 (ω=2).

**The "top quark" migrates to higher ω as N increases.**

### The Higgs Mechanism
The prime p=439 sits right next to k=440 = p+1 = 2³·5·11.
- p=439 (prime): weight 0.109 — an absurdly massive "boson"
- k=440 (composite): weight 0.139 — the second-heaviest "fermion"

This neighbor pair embodies the Higgs mechanism: the Higgs field (439)
gives mass to the fermion (440) through their proximity at the boundary.
Without the boundary, 439 would be massless like all other primes.

## Caveat: This is Speculative

The mapping above is **structural, not predictive**. The Standard Model
has exactly 17 fundamental particles. The Gram matrix at N=1000 has
168 primes and 831 composites. We cannot naturally reduce these to 17
without arbitrary choices.

What IS real:
- The boson/fermion role split (proved by the experiment)
- The mass hierarchy spanning many orders of magnitude
- The generation structure via ω(k)
- The Higgs-like boundary effect on large primes

What is NOT real:
- Any specific mapping of integer k to particle name
- Mass ratios that match GeV values
- The exact number of particles matching the SM count

The mapping is a pedagogical tool, not a physical prediction.
